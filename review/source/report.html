Abstract

The turbulent flow in the corner formed by two flat plates joined at right angles (an L-shaped wall–plate junction, 460 mm long, ReL = 1.1 × 106) is studied with steady RANS simulations (ANSYS Fluent, SST k–ω, y+ < 1). Compared with the mid-semester study, the domain, boundary conditions, mesh and solver were corrected. A three-grid study (0.53, 1.15 and 2.53 million cells, effective refinement ratio about 1.30) gives an estimated fine-grid GCI of 4.6% for the drag coefficient (Cd = 0.0617). For the corner velocity magnitude the GCI is 2.7%, but the apparent order (4.2) exceeds the formal order; limiting it to 2 gives about 8%, comparable to the 4.4% medium-to-fine change. The wake monitor is not in the asymptotic convergence range. The blunt leading edge produces a stagnation region strongest at the inside corner and a separation bubble that reattaches 40–77 mm downstream. Vorticity contours and cross-flow vectors show a counter-rotating corner pair whose plate-side cell is 1.1–1.9 times stronger, consistent with mean-flow tilting near the leading edge. At intermediate stations, fluid moves towards the corner along both walls and away along the bisector, while high-momentum fluid is brought towards the walls 15–30 mm from the corner. Skin friction is 30–50% lower in the corner. The total unsigned circulation in the sampled window decreases by about 9.5 times between the first and last cross-planes, mostly within the first 130 mm. The limitations of SST for stress-induced secondary flow and of a contour-based mechanism interpretation are discussed.

Chapter 1

Introduction and Literature Review

Wherever a body meets a wall—a wing on a fuselage, a fin on a hull, a pier on a river bed—the boundary layers of the two surfaces meet in a corner. This junction flow is three-dimensional: vortices form at the leading edge, cross-stream (secondary) motion appears along the corner, and the wall shear and drag there differ from those on either surface alone (Simpson, 2001). The project started from the experiment of Nakayama and Rahai (1984), who measured the mean and secondary velocities, Reynolds stresses and skin friction behind a flat plate mounted normal to a wall and found secondary motion that changes the wall shear near the junction.

Turbulent secondary flows are of two kinds (Bradshaw, 1987). Prandtl’s first kind arises when existing vorticity is tilted by the mean flow associated with geometry and pressure gradients. The horseshoe vortex around a wing–body junction, with legs trailing along the corners, is a classic example (Simpson, 2001). The second kind is driven by Reynolds-stress anisotropy in a straight corner (Gessner, 1973) and is typically weak, about 1–3% of the main velocity. Vorticity is solenoidal, ∇·ω = 0, so vortex lines cannot end within the fluid (Saffman, 1992). This is a kinematic constraint, not by itself proof of a particular tilting mechanism or vortex-line connection. The SST k–ω model (Menter, 1994) is widely used for wall-bounded flows with separation, but its linear eddy-viscosity closure cannot represent the stress-anisotropy mechanism responsible for classical second-kind secondary motion (Pope, 2000).

The mid-semester study was qualitative and unverified. The objectives of this phase were: (i) a corrected simulation of an L-shaped wall–plate junction, with iterative checks and a three-grid discretisation assessment (Celik et al., 2008); (ii) an interpretation of how the floor and plate boundary layers interact, using vorticity contours and secondary-flow vectors; and (iii) a quantitative comparison of the corner with regions far from it. The reference experiment provides qualitative context rather than a direct validation case because the incoming boundary layers differ.

2.1 Geometry, model and mesh

The L consists of a vertical plate and a floor plate, each 460 mm long, 320 mm wide and 10 mm thick, with square edges, inside a 2.0 × 0.60 × 0.50 m box. There is 0.77 m of free stream upstream and downstream. The inside corner runs along x = y = 0.01 m; LE and TE are at z = +0.23 and −0.23 m. Flow is in −z, with ReL = U∞L/ν = 1.13 × 106. The lateral enclosure faces use symmetry conditions, replacing the mid-semester duct walls that accelerated the flow to 41.8 m/s.

2.2 Analysis

Let s = 0.23 − z be distance downstream from the LE, d the distance normal to a wall, and U = |u| the exported velocity magnitude; u, v and w remain Cartesian components. Drag on all eight L faces (pressure and viscous contributions) is projected along (0, 0, −1): Cd = FD/(½ρU∞²Aref), with Aref = 0.46 × 0.32 = 0.1472 m², the side area of one plate. The corner and wake monitors are velocity magnitudes at (0.02, 0.02, 0) and (0.02, 0.02, −0.35) m.

At z = 0, profiles normal to each wall lie 240 mm (far) or 10 mm (near) from the other wall; a fifth profile follows the bisector. For far profiles, Ue is the mean for d > 35 mm and δ99 is interpolated at 0.99Ue. The script integrates the sampled profile to d = 50 mm with q = min[1, max(0, U/Ue)]: δ* = ∫(1 − q) dd and θ = ∫q(1 − q) dd. Thus these are finite-range, speed-based estimates; near profiles without a plateau are not assigned conventional thicknesses. Bisector distance is (x + y − 0.02)/√2.

Cross-planes at z = +0.20, +0.10, 0 and −0.20 m correspond to s = 30, 130, 230 and 430 mm. Exported nodal fields are linearly interpolated onto a 241 × 241 grid over 0–60 mm from each inner wall. Positive and negative ωz are integrated separately over finite samples, excluding the first 2 mm next to either wall. Wall comparisons use corner (0–4 mm), downwash (15–30 mm) and reference (60–150 mm) bands. Cf uses wall-shear magnitude divided by ½ρU∞²; the signed streamwise shear identifies reversal.

Drag changes by 1.43% from medium to fine, and the extrapolated value is 3.7% below the fine-grid value (Figure 3.2). GCI estimates are conditional because refinement is not uniform. For the corner monitor the medium-to-fine change is 4.4%; because the apparent order p = 4.2 exceeds the formal order of the scheme, the 2.7% GCI should be read as a lower bound, and limiting p to 2 (Roache, 1998) gives about 8%. Wake values decrease monotonically, but successive differences grow (1.407 to 1.562 m/s; ratio ≈ 1.11), so no reliable asymptotic GCI is obtained. Its 6.1% medium-to-fine difference is not a quantified uncertainty; the short outlet distance (Section 2.1) may contribute. Subsequent results use the fine grid.

3.2 Leading edge of the junction

The inlet total pressure is 819 Pa (794 Pa dynamic plus 25 Pa static). The node-averaged maximum pressure on the L is 837 Pa, 2% above the inlet total pressure. Since the static pressure cannot physically exceed the upstream total pressure, this overshoot is a local discretisation error at the sharp stagnation edge and indicates the numerical error there. Iso-surfaces place the largest stagnation region at the inside corner of the blunt front face, where both plates obstruct the oncoming fluid. The node-averaged peak speed is 1.24U∞; larger isolated cell peaks are not representative of the bulk flow. Acceleration around the square edges is followed by separation. Reversed floor shear persists 70–77 mm downstream from the LE far from the plate but only 40–50 mm within 30 mm of it (Figure 3.8a).

3.3 Boundary layers far from and near the junction

Far from the junction the boundary layers are alike at mid-length: δ99 = 25.6 and 26.0 mm, δ* = 6.12 and 6.34 mm, θ = 3.69 and 3.86 mm, and H = 1.66 and 1.64 for floor and plate respectively. These thicknesses exceed the flat-plate estimate 0.37s Res−1/5 ≈ 6 mm at s = 0.23 m (Schlichting and Gersten, 2017). H exceeds the equilibrium value 1.3–1.4, consistent with recovery after leading-edge separation. Ue ≈ 37.4 m/s is about 4% above the inlet speed. This speed-up is consistent with the combined blockage of the blunt edges (2.1% of the domain cross-section) and of the displacement thickness on the four wetted faces (about 1.28 m × 6.2 mm ≈ 2.6%).

Near-corner profiles are fuller close to the wall: about 28.4 m/s at d = 10 mm compared with 26.0–26.7 m/s far away. They do not reach an independent outer plateau because they remain in the other wall’s boundary layer. Along the bisector, 99% of Ue is reached at 27.3 mm, versus the geometric scale √2δ99 ≈ 36 mm. This point is only 27.3/√2 ≈ 19 mm from each wall, less than δ99, so the corner region carries more momentum than either boundary layer alone would suggest. This comparison is consistent with momentum redistribution by the secondary flow (Section 3.5), but is not a rigorous superposition model.

3.4 Vorticity at the corner

The floor boundary layer carries ωx < 0 and the plate boundary layer ωy > 0, with wall magnitudes of order 105 s−1. At z = 0, the ωx layer thins towards the plate and the ωy layer weakens towards the floor (Figure 3.4). This shows interaction between the two wall-vorticity distributions, but contours alone do not trace connected vortex lines.

For incompressible mean flow, the streamwise vorticity equation may be written schematically as

(u·∇)ωz = ωx ∂w/∂x + ωy ∂w/∂y + ωz ∂w/∂z + ν∇²ωz − [∇×(∇·R)]z,
(3.1)

where Rij = ⟨u′iu′j⟩ is the time-averaged Reynolds-stress tensor. The first two right-hand terms represent tilting, the third stretching, and the remaining terms viscous and turbulent-stress contributions. Independently, ∇·ω = 0 implies ∂ωz/∂z = −(∂ωx/∂x + ∂ωy/∂y).

The solenoidal condition constrains the spatial field but does not establish which transport term in Eq. (3.1) creates the corner vorticity. A term-by-term budget or vortex-line/pathline analysis was not performed. The interpretation of leading-edge tilting is therefore based on the joint evidence of vorticity, separation and cross-flow, rather than a measured transport balance.

Inside the leading-edge separation bubble (z = +0.20 m), ωz is strong and patchy. Farther downstream, positive vorticity remains predominantly beside the plate and negative vorticity above the floor, centred roughly 5–20 mm from the walls. The topology is consistent with a counter-rotating pair, but the two signed circulations are unequal, particularly at z = +0.10 m; the flow should not be described as quantitatively symmetric.

Figure 3.6 shows that most of the reduction occurs between s = 30 and 130 mm (a factor of 3.7 in total circulation), where the leading-edge separation bubble reattaches (Section 3.2); between s = 130 and 430 mm the total falls by a further factor of only 2.5. The plate-side cell (Γ+) is stronger at every station (Γ+/|Γ−| = 1.11–1.85). This is consistent with the shorter floor separation bubble near the plate and with the asymmetric skin-friction enhancement in Section 3.6.

The downstream reduction is consistent with weakening and redistribution of leading-edge-generated vorticity. Diffusion may contribute, but flux through the finite window, convection, stretching and tilting have not been separated. The result therefore supports a leading-edge-related, first-kind secondary-flow interpretation without proving that turbulent diffusion alone causes the decay.

3.5 Secondary flow and mixing in the corner

Figure 3.7 shows the cross-flow associated with the corner pair. At z = +0.10 m and 0, vectors within approximately 3 mm of each wall point towards the corner (0.1–0.46 m/s), while vectors along the bisector point away from it (0.07–0.28 m/s). Low-momentum fluid is swept along the walls into the corner and lifted along the bisector. On the outer side of each cell, faster fluid is brought towards the wall about 15–30 mm from the corner. The floor-side circulation has the same sense as a horseshoe-vortex leg (Simpson, 2001). The bisector outflow is weak and confined close to the corner; over the corner region as a whole the high-momentum downwash on either side dominates, which is why the near-corner profiles in Figure 3.3 are fuller than the far profiles.

The isovels bend towards the walls beside the corner, consistent with the fuller near-wall profiles in Figure 3.3 and the shorter bisector distance to 99% of the edge speed. High-momentum transport is towards the walls on the downwash sides of the cells; it is not directed into the corner along the bisector, where the intermediate-plane vectors point outward.

At z = +0.20 m the maximum cross-flow in the sampled window is about 3.5 m/s (nearly 10% of U∞) and is dominated by outward displacement from the blunt leading edge. At z = −0.20 m the cross-flow converges towards the walls ahead of the trailing edge. Thus the intermediate-plane circulation pattern should not be extrapolated unchanged to every streamwise station.

These velocity and vorticity fields demonstrate mean momentum redistribution. They do not quantify turbulent mixing rates or resolve unsteady vortex shedding; the calculation is steady RANS, and the remaining iterative oscillations are not a physical time history.

3.6 Wall shear stress

The secondary flow leaves a clear imprint on skin friction (Figure 3.8). Within 4 mm of the corner, Cf is about 30–50% lower than in the reference band 60–150 mm away. For example, on the floor at z = +0.10 m it is 1.05 × 10−3, compared with 2.16 × 10−3 in the reference band. Part of this reduction is geometric: the velocity gradient, and hence the wall shear, vanishes towards a 90° corner because both walls retard the flow. The secondary flow modifies the distribution around this, sweeping low-momentum fluid along the walls into the corner and producing the downwash band of increased Cf.

In the 15–30 mm downwash band, the floor Cf is 2.86 × 10−3 at z = +0.10 m, 32% above the reference-band mean; the plate increase there is about 13%. The floor enhancement declines to approximately 12% at z = 0 and 8% at z = −0.20 m, while the corresponding plate changes are about 0% and 2%. These differences are consistent with the unequal vortex strengths (Figure 3.6b). Away from the corner, Cf increases downstream as the boundary layer recovers after separation.

Relation to the reference experiment. In Nakayama and Rahai (1984), a developed wall boundary layer arrives from upstream. Here both wall boundary layers start at the same blunt LE. The comparison is therefore qualitative: both flows show streamwise corner vorticity, momentum redistribution and local wall-shear changes. Agreement of these trends does not establish quantitative validation against that experiment.

Chapter 4

Conclusions and Future Work

  1. 1. The corrected L-junction RANS model satisfies y+ < 1 and global mass balance, with bounded final-iteration monitor variation. The fine-grid drag coefficient is Cd = 0.06165; its estimated discretisation GCI is 4.6% and the extrapolated value is 3.7% lower. The corner-speed GCI is 2.7%, but this relies on an apparent order (4.2) above the formal order; limiting the order to 2 gives about 8%, comparable to the 4.4% medium-to-fine change. These are discretisation estimates, not total uncertainties or experimental validation.
  2. 2. The wake-monitor velocity decreases monotonically from coarse to fine, but grid differences do not contract. The 6.1% medium-to-fine difference is a sensitivity measure, not a reliable uncertainty estimate; further local refinement and a longer downstream domain are needed for a converged wake value.
  3. 3. The blunt LE produces the strongest stagnation region at the inside corner and a shorter separation bubble near the junction (40–50 mm) than far from it (70–77 mm). The mid-length layers remain much thicker than an equilibrium flat-plate estimate.
  4. 4. Vorticity contours and cross-flow vectors indicate a counter-rotating corner pair with unequal strengths; the plate-side cell is 1.1–1.9 times stronger at every station. The total unsigned circulation in the sampled window decreases about 9.5 times between s = 30 and 430 mm, by a factor of 3.7 already within the first 130 mm. Leading-edge tilting is a plausible interpretation; neither a complete vorticity budget nor vortex-line connectivity was demonstrated.
  5. 5. At intermediate stations, low-momentum fluid moves towards the corner along the walls and away along the bisector, while faster fluid moves towards the walls 15–30 mm away, so that the corner region as a whole is fuller than either boundary layer alone. Corner skin friction is 30–50% lower than the reference band, partly for geometric reasons; the adjacent floor enhancement reaches 32% and diminishes downstream.

Limitations and future work. SST does not represent the Reynolds-stress-anisotropy mechanism of second-kind corner flow. Cross-flow is about 10% of U∞ near the LE but of order 1% downstream, where this model limitation matters more. Model-form and discretisation errors are separate. The domain has an outlet only 1.7L behind the TE and a top boundary about 80 mm above the plate edge, and about 0.1% of cells have low orthogonal quality at the outer floor edge. Future work includes an SST corner-flow correction or Reynolds-stress model, refinement of the corner wake with a longer downstream domain, pathlines, vortex lines and wake-recovery profiles, a pressure/viscous decomposition of the drag, an incoming wall boundary layer for closer experimental comparison, and the NACA 0010 wing–body junction. These extensions were not carried out in the present study.
